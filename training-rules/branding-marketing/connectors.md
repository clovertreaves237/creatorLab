# Connector Rules — Transition Language

## What a Connector Is

A connector is one sentence — sometimes just a phrase — that sits between
two sections of a script. Its only job is to make the viewer choose to stay
for the next beat. It is not a summary of what just happened. It is not a
heading. It is a pull forward.

Think of it as the cliff-hanger at the end of a TV episode. Small, specific,
and it makes leaving feel like a loss.

---

## The One Rule That Overrides Everything

**If the connector does not make the viewer need to hear what comes next,
cut it. A clean cut is always better than a weak bridge.**

Silence, a pause, or a direct jump to the next idea is stronger than a
filler transition. Weak connectors are the number one reason viewers tap
away between beats.

---

## The Three Jobs a Connector Can Do

Every connector must do exactly one of these. If it does none, delete it.

**1. Open a question the viewer does not yet have the answer to**
The viewer's brain stays engaged because it is now looking for the answer.

**2. Create a gap between what the viewer knows and what they want to know**
You hint that something is missing from their understanding. They stay to
fill the gap.

**3. Signal that a payoff is coming in the very next sentence**
You make a specific promise that the next beat will deliver on immediately.
Do not promise and then delay. If you say "here is the fix," the fix must
be the next sentence.

---

## Approved Connector Patterns

These are structures, not scripts. Write them in your own voice.
Do not paste these word for word into a script.

### Pattern 1 — The Question Bridge
Closes a beat by asking the question the next beat answers.

Examples:
- "But here is the part nobody talks about."
- "So why does this keep happening?"
- "And that is exactly where it falls apart."
- "The question is — what do you do with that?"

### Pattern 2 — The Contrast Shift
Sets up a tension between two ideas. Works well between a common belief
and a reframe.

Examples:
- "That works for established brands. It does not work for you — yet."
- "On paper that makes sense. In practice it is the opposite."
- "Most people hear this and stop. The ones who win keep going."
- "Sounds simple. It is not. Here is why."

### Pattern 3 — The Specific Promise
Names exactly what is coming next. The specificity is what creates trust
and keeps the viewer watching.

Examples:
- "There is one shift that changes all of this. It takes about ten seconds
  to understand."
- "Here is what actually works — and I will show you in the next thirty
  seconds."
- "There are two reasons this happens. The second one is the one that
  matters."

### Pattern 4 — The Reframe Bridge
Redefines what the viewer just heard before moving to the next idea.
Used when you want the viewer to hold the previous beat differently.

Examples:
- "So it is not really about the content. It is about what the content
  signals."
- "The tool is not the problem. This is."
- "That is not a strategy problem. That is a clarity problem."

### Pattern 5 — The Unexpected Turn
Works when the next beat contradicts or complicates what came before.
Creates curiosity by making the viewer feel the previous beat was
incomplete.

Examples:
- "Except that is not the full picture."
- "Here is where it gets interesting."
- "And then everything I just said has one exception."

---

## Connectors to Never Use

These phrases slow the script down and signal to the viewer that nothing
important is coming. Remove every single one on sight.

- "So anyway..."
- "Moving on..."
- "The next thing I want to talk about is..."
- "As I mentioned earlier..."
- "Let me explain..."
- "With that being said..."
- "So basically..."
- "In conclusion..."
- "To summarise..."
- Any connector that starts with "I" — it shifts focus from the viewer
  to the creator
- Any connector longer than fifteen words
- Any rhetorical question where the viewer already knows the answer
  ("Pretty important, right?")
- Filler affirmations ("Right, so..." / "OK so..." / "Yeah so...")

---

## Placement Rules

- **One connector** between the Hook and the first Body beat — maximum
- **One connector** between Body beats — only when the jump between ideas
  is large enough to need it
- **Zero connectors** before the Close. Cut directly into it. The momentum
  of the body carries the viewer to the close on its own
- Never stack two connectors back to back. If you feel the need to,
  the problem is the body beat structure, not the connector
- In scripts under 45 seconds: aim for one connector total, possibly zero
- In scripts 60–120 seconds: two connectors maximum

---

## Connector Length by Script Duration

| Script length     | Max connector length |
|-------------------|----------------------|
| Under 30 seconds  | 5 words              |
| 30–60 seconds     | 8 words              |
| 60–120 seconds    | 12 words             |

If you need more words than this to transition, the previous beat did not
land cleanly. Fix the beat, not the connector.

---

## Tone Matching

A connector must feel like it belongs in the same conversation as the rest
of the script. If the script is direct and confident, the connector must be
direct and confident. If the niche file sets a warmer tone, the connector
softens to match.

The connector is not where you change gear. It is where you hold gear.

Each niche pack will specify vocabulary preferences for connectors in that
niche. When a niche rule conflicts with a core rule, the niche rule wins.

## How to Read These Examples

Every example is built from a real handoff between two sections in the branding and marketing transcripts (Transcripts 1–15, in order). Each one is a standalone option, not a sequence: in a real script you would use one connector between the Hook and Body 1 at most, and one between Body beats only when the jump is large.

Every STRONG connector is tagged with the guide rules it follows:

- **Placement** — Hook → Body 1, or Body → Body (never before the Close)
- **Pattern** — one of the five approved patterns in `connectors.md`
- **Job** — the one job it does: open a question, create a gap, or signal an immediate payoff
- **Length tier** — the maximum words allowed for the script duration it fits

Each STRONG connector is a single sentence or phrase that does not recap the previous beat, and it never starts with "I". These are structures to learn from, so write your own in your own voice rather than pasting them.

## When the Best Connector Is None

The guide's override rule: if a connector does not make the viewer need what comes next, cut it. Before the Close there are zero connectors, because the momentum of the body carries the viewer in.

## What the Strong Versions Have in Common

| Pattern | What It Does | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Question Bridge** | Closes a beat by asking the question the next beat answers | 15 |
| **Contrast Shift** | Sets two ideas against each other so the next beat resolves the tension | 6, 7, 8, 13, 32, 44, 58 |
| **Specific Promise** | Names exactly what is coming, which creates trust | 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 10, 11, 14, 16, 18, 24, 25, 26, 30, 31, 34, 38, 40, 42, 45, 47, 49, 50, 55, 56, 57, 60, 61, 64, 66, 68 |
| **Reframe Bridge** | Changes how the viewer holds what they just heard | 19, 22, 23, 27, 36, 43, 48, 51, 63 |
| **Unexpected Turn** | Makes the previous beat feel incomplete | 9, 12, 17, 20, 21, 28, 29, 33, 35, 37, 39, 41, 46, 52, 53, 54, 59, 62, 65, 67, 69 |

## Final Check Before Locking a Script

Read every connector out loud. Ask one question for each:

> **"If I cut this sentence, does the viewer lose anything they need?"**

If the answer is no — cut it.
